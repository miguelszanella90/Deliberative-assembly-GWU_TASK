# Appendix A — Deliberative Assembly: Game Rules and Experimental Protocol

**Research question.** What is the effect of continuous inductive learning on collective reasoning and decision-making among randomly assigned participants in controlled, scheduled deliberative sessions, where reasoning capacities, communication opportunities, and power relationships vary under conditions of time pressure and uncertainty?

**Core construct.** Continuous Inductive Learning is the ongoing presentation, exchange, consideration, and potential integration of relevant inductive information during repeated deliberative sessions. It does not imply that policy quality must improve in every round.

## Purpose and format

Deliberative Assembly is a controlled, digital legislative decision-making game designed as a pilot instrument for the proposed research. Each experiment consists of **seven rounds** with **ten participants**. In the assisted pilot, **P1 is human-controlled and P2–P10 are simulated legislators**. A future study would assign independent participant assemblies randomly to experimental conditions. Players deliberate over fictional public-policy proposals and make observable choices about policy wording, evidence disclosure, persuasion, and voting.

## Experimental conditions

**Low-information control (Experiment 1, alternating on odd-numbered pilot games):** Six rounds provide no additional public external-evidence statements; one randomly placed round supplies one statement. Each player is scheduled to receive two private-information cards across seven rounds.

**High-information treatment (Experiment 2, alternating on even-numbered pilot games):** Six rounds present two additional public external-evidence statements; one randomly placed round supplies one statement. Each player is scheduled to receive five private-information cards across seven rounds.

The policy-case pool and institutional rules are shared between conditions, while case order and institutional circumstances vary. The medium-information round is rare by design. The game alternates control and treatment in the assisted pilot; in the proposed independent-assembly experiment the research design calls for random assignment, not deterministic alternation. Evidence availability, disclosure, use, and agreement with the benchmark are distinct measures.

## Parties, roles, and motivations

Each of ten legislators belongs to a fixed **Blue** or **Red** caucus (five per caucus). Players hold fixed assigned incentive profiles: **Collective**, **Party**, or **Individual**. At each round, the game assigns institutional roles: a **President**, a **Blue caucus leader**, a **Red caucus leader**, and one **nonvoting institutional Expert**. The President selects the final word in the policy bill; caucus leaders may issue YES/NO directives and exert pressure; legislators may comply or dissent. The expert presents or challenges information and cannot cast a legislative vote. Bills have a Blue, Red, or Joint sponsor. Conditions such as time pressure, communication restrictions, presidential authority, and institutional friction are varied.

## Seven-stage decision sequence

1. **Initial policy judgment.** Review the fictional proposal. Choose one of three candidate words; rate confidence and select a reason for the initial choice.
2. **Private information.** If assigned a card, inspect its relevance and fictional source, then explicitly select **Reveal** or **Withhold** and identify a reason. A player without a card proceeds without a disclosure decision.
3. **Deliberation.** Review the institutional setting and fellow legislators' initial positions. Choose an argument, target a legislator, and select an approach (evidence, compromise, or coalition-building). Interventions and simulated responses are recorded.
4. **Revised policy judgment.** Review information that was shared and any additional public external evidence. Keep or revise the policy word, update confidence, and explain the decision. In rounds 2–7 the interface asks about previous learning after deliberation and before the revised judgment.
5. **Presidential wording.** The President selects a final policy word, which is not necessarily the assembly's most popular choice. The final complete bill is shown for voting.
6. **Institutional directives and vote.** Once final wording is known, eligible leaders can issue caucus/presidential requests or briefings. Members may attend or decline a requested briefing (declining costs 2 personal points), then eligible legislators independently choose YES or NO. A preview displays potential point outcomes; it does not determine the vote. The expert abstains by institutional rule.
7. **Results and reflection.** A bill passes with **at least five YES votes among nine eligible legislators**. The game reveals the outcome, bipartisan support, scores, a predefined fictional evidence benchmark, and a learning reflection. Players may select multiple lessons and add an optional note; the next round can use a mid-round transfer question.

## Information sources

Evidence is deliberately fictional, coming from five source families: **Executive policy assessments**, **decided judicial cases**, **comparative-policy studies**, **university behavioral research**, and **legislative impact evaluations**. Cards may favor, challenge, or complicate a policy option and can differ in relevance. The program records which evidence was made available, shared, and reported as influential. No source is represented as a verified real-world empirical study.

## Points and competition

- **Personal scoring:** +4 when a player's revised word agrees with the case's predefined evidence-supported benchmark; +3 when the player's vote matches the final bill outcome; +4 for YES on an aligned bill that passes or NO on a non-aligned bill that fails; +2 for YES on a bipartisan passed bill; +2 for YES on a passed bill sponsored by their party or jointly sponsored.
- **Directive incentives:** During caucus-pressure conditions, eligible non-leaders receive +2 for following the caucus directive or −4 for defying it. During presidential-discretion conditions, eligible non-Presidents receive +2 for following the President's directive or −3 for defying it. Declining a briefing costs −2 personal points. Experts do not receive legislative-vote points.
- **Party scoring:** A passed Blue/Red-sponsored bill awards +10 party points to the sponsor; a passed jointly sponsored bill awards +5 to each party. Bipartisan passage adds +10 party points to both parties.
- **Presidency scoring:** The President gains +10 when a bill passes and a further +10 if the passage receives YES votes from both parties.

**Bipartisan support** means at least one YES vote from each party; **bipartisan passage** additionally requires that the bill pass. The three scoreboards are personal, party, and presidential. There is no automatic victory before the seventh round.

## Records and safeguards

The game records initial and revised preferences, confidence, private evidence assignment/disclosure, deliberative interventions, presidential decisions, institutional requests, eligible votes, scores, information conditions, learning reflections, and relevant event timestamps/order. The player-round, round-summary, event-log, and combined exports include **experiment identifiers** to support comparisons across complete seven-round sessions. A new experiment preserves archived sessions in the same browser when storage is available; **Delete ALL Experiments & Restart** explicitly clears that browser-local archive after confirmation. Previously downloaded research files are not deleted.

**Pilot status:** Nine of the ten legislators are algorithmically simulated, and pilot results serve to demonstrate the proposed randomized experimental design, interface, and measurement procedure. The human-participant proposal calls for independently randomized control and treatment assemblies. Fictional case benchmarks are operational measures of policy alignment, not claims of objective real-world legislative quality.
